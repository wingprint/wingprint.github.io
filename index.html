The idea

Identify a bat by the print already on its wing.

Bats are small, nocturnal and fast, with few stable external markings — so tracking individuals has traditionally meant catching, banding or tagging them, with real welfare and logistical costs. WingPrint reads a marker the animal already carries: the pattern woven into its wing membrane.

That pattern stays recognisable over time and even after injuries heal, which makes it a durable biometric. We built two ways to capture it — a handheld scanner for bats in the hand, and an in-flight camera trap for free-flying bats at a feeder — and paired both with automated matching, so identification can scale from a single field season to continuous monitoring.

Two instruments

In the hand during capture, in mid-air for nectar-feeding species.

The manual scanner

A rotating, transparent acrylic plate over an infrared source, with a camera above. The operator extends one wing onto the glass; the plate tilts through 45° while the camera records the membrane from 18 angles in about 20 seconds. It drops into standard capture workflows — used across three field seasons in Panama.

  • Illumination850 nm infrared backlight
  • HandlingHeld briefly
  • Species tested38 species · 7 families
  • Re-ID methodKeypoint matching

The in-flight camera trap

A feeding station with infrared backlighting and a high-speed camera. When a bat inserts its snout, a light barrier triggers the camera, which records the spread wing at 238 fps as the animal hovers, capturing 140 frames per approach — no capture, no handling, no marking. Bats simply visit the feeder.

  • HandlingNone · free flight
  • Species testedGlossophaga mutica

Two pipelines

The matching method follows the data available per individual.

Manual scanner · classical CV

Keypoint matching

Handheld scans are matched with classical computer vision — no training data needed.

  1. Crop the ROIA YOLO pose model locates the forearm and crops the plagiopatagium, where veins and collagen are most distinctive.
  2. EnhanceResize to 400×400 and boost local contrast with CLAHE so the fine fibre structure stands out.
  3. Match keypointsRootSIFT descriptors are compared to the gallery and geometrically verified with RANSAC.
  4. Assign by inliersThe gallery entry sharing the most consistent keypoints wins — or the scan is flagged as a new individual.
In-flight · deep learning

Deep metric learning

In-flight frames are learned into an embedding space where each individual forms its own cluster.

  1. Select framesFrom each approach, only frames with a fully extended, well-lit wing are kept.
  2. EmbedAn EfficientNetV2 network trained with ArcFace turns each wing into a compact feature vector.
  3. CompareQuery embeddings are ranked against the gallery by cosine similarity.
  4. Assign or rejectThe nearest identity is returned; anything below a threshold is rejected as unknown — enabling open-world use.

Applied re-identification

Validated in a behavioural experiment.

We used the in-flight scanner to study individual foraging and competition at a programmable nectar feeder. Under a replenishing reward regime, nectar volume increased from 0 to 85 µl over three minutes, so that visits paid off differently depending on when a bat arrived.

All 2,215 feeder visits of one night were attributed to individuals automatically, starting from an empty gallery and without any prior capture or marking; 97.2 % were assigned to the correct animal. Nectar intake differed strongly between individuals: one bat achieved the highest volume per visit, while another obtained the largest total intake through 874 visits. The recordings also captured aggressive interactions at the feeder, with one individual repeatedly displacing others.
